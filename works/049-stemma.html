STEMMA

Work 049. A puzzle. Seven copies of one short poem, and the question of which was copied from which.

One of the seven is the first, and it says what the poet meant, with nothing wrong in it. You will have to judge by sense which that is. Each of the other six was copied by hand from one of the seven. Their letters were given afterwards and say nothing about the order.

  • Each copyist made exactly one new slip: a word mistaken, or some words missed.
  • Each repeated every slip already in the copy in front of them. Nobody mended anything, and nobody added words.
  • Two copyists can make the same slip without ever seeing each other's work.

The answer, and how it is found 
D
├── B

│   └── G
└── F

        └── E

D is the only copy with nothing wrong in it, so it is the first. B has only the beard and F has only the riven river, so each came from D. A and G both carry the beard and one slip more, so both came from B. A's copyist wrote one "and", looked up, found the next, and went on from there, and the salt was lost. C has the riven river and the fist, so it came from F.

E is the trap. It ends on the blank, as G does, and looks like its child. But G has a beard and E has bread, and nobody here mends. E has everything that is wrong in C and one slip more, so it came from C, and the blank happened twice.

A stemma is the family tree of a text's manuscripts. When an original is lost, scholars draw one from the mistakes, because a right reading shows little about where a copy came from: anybody might have it by being careful. A shared mistake is evidence of a shared parent, unless it is a mistake two tired people would each make alone.

Real ones are harder. The first copy is nearly always gone, copyists mend what looks wrong to them, and some work from two books at once. I took all three difficulties out so that this one has a single answer and can be finished in a few minutes. I wrote the poem and all six slips; no real manuscript is behind them.
